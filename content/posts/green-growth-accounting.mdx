Green growth is often summarized by a pleasing ratio: more output, fewer emissions. That ratio matters, but it can conceal whether an economy is building the capabilities required to sustain the transition.

## Beyond carbon intensity

A country can lower territorial emissions by importing carbon-intensive goods, postponing infrastructure maintenance, or losing industrial capacity. None is a durable development strategy. A stronger accounting framework needs to track how production changes, who bears adjustment costs, and whether new capabilities are accumulating.

Three additional dimensions deserve attention:

- **Resilience:** exposure to energy-price, climate, and supply-chain shocks.
- **Capability:** domestic knowledge, skills, and firms able to deliver transition technologies.
- **Distribution:** employment, household welfare, and regional adjustment effects.

## Measure the transition path

Static indicators compare endpoints. Policy happens along a path. The same emissions reduction can result from productive reallocation or economic contraction, and those mechanisms imply very different futures.

Researchers should pair outcome indicators with transition accounts: capital formation by technology, worker movement across sectors, electricity reliability, and the composition of trade.

> Decarbonization is not only a subtraction problem. It is an institution-building and capability-building project.

## A more useful dashboard

The objective is not to replace GDP or emissions. It is to create a balanced evidence base that makes tradeoffs visible. Good green-growth accounting should tell policymakers whether an economy is cleaner today—and whether it is becoming more capable of staying that way.
