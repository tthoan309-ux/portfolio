Public programs routinely ask people to reveal far more than is necessary: entire documents to prove a threshold, permanent identifiers to prevent duplicate claims, or detailed records to establish eligibility. Modern cryptography offers a different institutional design.

## Verification without disclosure

A zero-knowledge proof can demonstrate that a statement is true without revealing the underlying private data. In public policy, the statement might be that income lies below a threshold, a credential came from an approved issuer, or a benefit has not already been claimed.

This is not merely a technical improvement. It changes the relationship between citizens and administrative systems by reducing the amount of trust—and retained personal data—required for a transaction.

## Where economics enters

Privacy infrastructure has costs: implementation, governance, usability, and the possibility of new exclusion when systems become too complex. Its value therefore depends on institutional context.

Economic analysis can help answer:

- Which verification steps create the highest privacy risk relative to their fraud-prevention value?
- Who pays the fixed cost of adopting shared proof infrastructure?
- How should liability be allocated when issuers, wallets, and verifiers fail?

## Treat it as public infrastructure

The largest gains may come from interoperable standards rather than isolated applications. Shared credential formats and auditable open implementations can reduce duplication across agencies while preserving contestability.

> Privacy should not be a premium feature of public administration.

Cryptography will not fix weak institutions. But used carefully, it can make good institutional principles—data minimization, proportionality, and accountable verification—easier to implement at scale.
